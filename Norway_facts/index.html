Stunning landscape

Norway is packed with unique and stunning nature to see.
Whether there are fjords in the Vestlands, unique-shaped stone formations over sea or lakes, magic Lofoten, Aurora Borealis, midnight sun - Norway is the perfect travel destination all around the year.
Here, I want to share my experience with this country.

My Stories

Trolltunga, together with Preikestolen, is one of the most famous rock formations in Norway.
I have been to Trolltunga three times, each time in a different season and with different people.
The first time was in early spring, in April. The snow had started to melt and there was water running everywhere, but there were still large heaps and patches of snow along the way. I was hiking with a Dutch guy, and another man from Indonesia joined us. He was travelling around Europe and, apparently, it was the first time in his life that he had ever stood on snow or touched it. I couldn't believe it, but he seemed so happy, posing for several photos on a heap of snow with his thumbs up.
During that trip, we slept at a campsite near Hardangervidda and also visited the Folgefonna glacier.
The second time I went to Trolltunga was in October with two Czech friends. The weather was grey, a little rainy and quite misty. However, we could still see about twenty metres ahead of us.
The third time was in late June, and the weather was absolutely perfect — sunny and clear. I went there with a Slovak couple and a Norwegian girl. This trip was quite amusing.
By then, a new road had been opened, so we didn't have to climb all the stairs next to the old funicular. On the way to Trolltunga, we even met a donkey with four human legs! We also saw several marriage proposals, with people getting down on one knee and offering a ring on the rock. We met a few trolls as well.
The Norwegian girl who was with us was picking up rubbish along the trail because she wanted to do something environmentally friendly while hiking.
You should reserve a whole day for a trip to Trolltunga. The round trip from Skjeggedal takes about eight hours. The trail is quite steep during the first two hours, but after that the landscape becomes flatter and more like a typical Norwegian vidda, or mountain plateau.
Some people even camp in the area and spend the night surrounded by the beautiful mountain landscape.

I visited Stavanger and the surrounding area four times during my stay in Norway. In Stavanger, there is a central lake and colourful wooden houses with typical Western Norwegian architecture. There are also beaches with swans and prehistoric rock carvings in Austre Åmøy, dating back around 2,000–3,000 years. It is also worth visiting a place called Swords in Rock, especially at dusk, when you can take beautiful photos.
Near Stavanger, there are many beautiful places to visit. I considered them a must-see while I was in Norway. The most visited places in Rogaland are Preikestolen and Kjerag, both of which are impressive rock formations above Lysefjord. To get to Preikestolen, you can travel from Stavanger by boat and continue from the other side. You can also take a boat trip on Lysefjord and see Preikestolen from the water, together with beautiful waterfalls cascading down the cliffs. While Preikestolen is relatively close to Stavanger, Kjerag is farther away and takes more than an hour to reach by bus.

I travelled to Vesterålen in Northern Norway. There was a lot of snow, unusually shaped mountains and the sea. It was extremely cold, and we had to drive carefully because of the winter conditions. We even saw a moose eating leaves. We also visited a farm with llamas and fed them.
There was a sauna and a beach near the place where we were staying, and it was actually a very good spot for watching the Northern Lights. Most of the time, we only saw weak, dispersed aurora because it was cloudy. However, one night we saw beautiful stripes of light and a dancing aurora in the sky. That was many years ago.
Later, I also visited Alta, where I saw a very weak aurora with a few stripes here and there. There was a full moon that night, and it was also very cloudy.
Finally, I visited Tromsø with my boyfriend at the time. There, we saw a moderately strong aurora forming curtains and stripes above the mountains. We could also see some purple aurora beneath the green lights. To see the Northern Lights at their best, you often have to hunt for a good spot and drive outside the city to dark areas with clear skies. We saw green arcs and curtains of aurora moving away from us and slowly fading into the night sky.

Yes, I have been there several times. Oslo is the capital of Norway. I visited Frogner Park, where there are more than 200 sculptures of naked people with expressive faces, all created by Gustav Vigeland. One place that is definitely still on my bucket list is the MUNCH museum in Bjørvika, at Edvard Munchs plass 1. The museum houses several versions of Edvard Munch’s famous The Scream, and one version is always on display. The painting shows a figure under a dramatic red sky above the Oslofjord.

In Ibsen’s Peer Gynt, Peer rides a reindeer buck along Gjendineggen, the name Ibsen used for what is now called Besseggen. You can really get quite close to reindeer in this area, although they are shy and usually keep their distance rather than coming towards you.


List of Norwegian UNESCO Heritage

Bryggen, the old wharf of Bergen, is a reminder of the town’s importance as part of the Hanseatic League’s trading empire from the 14th to the mid-16th century. Many fires, the last in 1955, have ravaged the characteristic wooden houses of Bryggen. Its rebuilding has traditionally followed old patterns and methods, thus leaving its main structure preserved, which is a relic of an ancient wooden urban structure once common in Northern Europe.
The wooden stave church of Urnes stands in the natural setting of Sogn og Fjordane. It was built in the 12th and 13th centuries and is an outstanding example of traditional Scandinavian wooden architecture. It brings together traces of Celtic art, Viking traditions and Romanesque spatial structures.
Røros Mining Town and the so called "Circumference" are linked to the copper mines, established in the 17th century and exploited for 333 years until 1977. The site comprises the Town and its industrial-rural cultural landscapes; Femundshytta, a smelter with its associated area; and the Winter Transport Route. Completely rebuilt after its destruction by Swedish troops in 1679, Røros contains about 2000 wooden one- and two-storey houses and a smelting house. Many of these buildings have preserved their blackened wooden façades, giving the town a medieval appearance. Surrounded by a buffer zone, coincident with the area of privileges (the Circumference) granted to the mining enterprise by the Danish-Norwegian Crown (1646), the property illustrates the establishment and flourishing of a lasting culture based on copper mining in a remote region with a harsh climate.
This group of petroglyphs in the Alta Fjord, near the Arctic Circle, shows traces of a settlement dating from c. 4200 to 500 B.C. The thousands of paintings and engravings add to our understanding of the environment and human activities on the fringes of the Far North in prehistoric times.
A cluster of islands centered on Vega, just south of the Arctic Circle, forms a cultural landscape of 103,710 ha, of which 6,930 ha is land. The islands bear testimony to a distinctive frugal way of life, based on fishing and harvesting down from the eider ducks over the past 1,500 years. Fishing villages, quays, warehouses, eider houses (built for eider ducks to nest in), farming landscapes, lighthouses and beacons are important sites. Evidence is also found of human settlement from the Stone Age onwards. By the 9th century, the islands had become an important centre for the supply of down, which appears to have accounted for around a third of the islanders’ income.
The Struve Geodetic Arc is a UNESCO World Heritage Site connected with one of the first accurate measurements of a long section of the Earth’s meridian. It was named after the astronomer Friedrich Georg Wilhelm Struve, who led the surveying work. Between 1816 and 1855, scientists created a chain of survey points stretching for more than 2,820 km from Hammerfest in Norway to the Black Sea. Their measurements helped scientists determine the size and shape of the Earth more accurately and were also an important example of international scientific cooperation. The original network contained 265 main survey points, but UNESCO protects 34 of them in ten different countries. Four of these points are in Norway: Fuglenes, Raipas, Luvdiidcohkka and Baelljasvarri. Fuglenes is a place in Hammerfest, where there is now a monument marking the northern end of the Struve Geodetic Arc.
The Nærøyfjord, one of the most beautiful and wildest arms of the Sognefjord, is listed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Fjord cruises take you through a unique landscape of sheer, snow-capped mountains, waterfalls and idyllic farms clinging to the mountainsides. The Nærøyfjord is 17 km long, and at its narrowest point it is only about 250 metres wide. Travelling through the Nærøyfjord is one of the most dramatic fjord journeys in Europe. Passenger boats operate between Gudvangen, Aurland and Flåm throughout the year.
The Geirangerfjord is a fjord in the Sunnmøre region of Møre og Romsdal county, Norway. It lies entirely within Stranda Municipality. It is a 15-kilometre-long branch of Sunnylvsfjorden, which itself is a branch of Storfjorden. The fjord is about 260 metres deep, while the surrounding mountains rise to 1,600–1,700 metres. It is also famous for its spectacular waterfalls and abandoned fjord farms high on the steep cliffsides. These now-deserted farms tell the story of a very different time and way of life. The idyllic landscape and dramatic mountains make the Geirangerfjord one of the most impressive places to visit in Norway.
The Rjukan–Notodden industrial site is an important example of Norway’s early industrialization. At the beginning of the 20th century, physicist **Kristian Birkeland** and engineer and entrepreneur **Sam Eyde** developed a special electric arc furnace for producing fertilizer from nitrogen in the air. Flowing water from rivers and waterfalls was used to drive turbines connected to electric generators. The electricity produced was then supplied to electrodes inside the furnace. A very powerful electric arc formed between the electrodes, creating an extremely hot plasma. Birkeland used a strong magnetic field to spread the arc into a broad, hot plasma disk, and air was blown through it. At such high temperatures, nitrogen and oxygen from the air could react. The resulting nitrogen oxides were later converted into nitric acid and finally into fertilizer. This technology helped transform Rjukan and Notodden into major industrial centres. Hydroelectric power plants, factories, railways and workers’ housing were built, and the new industry provided jobs for thousands of people. The UNESCO site therefore represents both an important technological achievement and a major stage in Norway’s industrial development.

Aurora borealis


The Aurora Borealis, commonly known as the Northern Lights, are glowing displays of green and occasionally other colors in the night sky, caused by charged particles entering Earth's atmosphere. This phenomenon occurs predominantly in the northern polar regions. In the southern hemisphere, a similar display is known as Aurora Australis.

The aurora phenomenon originates from the continuous stream of charged particles emitted by the Sun, known as solar wind. This solar wind consists primarily of plasma—electrons, protons, and ions—traveling through space and interacting with the magnetic fields of planets. Earth's magnetosphere, the region dominated by Earth's magnetic field, is significantly distorted by solar wind pressure. On the side facing the Sun (dayside), the magnetosphere is compressed to approximately 65,000 kilometers from Earth, while on the nightside, it is elongated to distances up to around 6,300,000 kilometers. This distortion creates regions near the poles with lower magnetic field density, forming funnel-like structures. Charged particles from space are funneled down these magnetic field lines into Earth's atmosphere due to voltage differences.

The aurora is produced primarily by charged particles initially captured and stored within the elongated tail of Earth's magnetosphere. These particles are then accelerated back toward Earth along magnetic field lines by strong electric potential differences between the magnetic tail and polar regions. Electrons, driven by these electromagnetic forces, collide with atoms and molecules in Earth's upper atmosphere, resulting in auroral emissions. (Learn more about Coronal Mass Ejections, or CMEs, here.)

The Sun completes a full rotation approximately every 27 days. This rotation influences the intensity and speed of the solar wind. Variations in solar wind conditions cause fluctuations in the energy of particles interacting with Earth's magnetic field. When solar activity decreases, Earth's magnetosphere experiences periods of relative calm, encountering fewer and slower-moving charged particles. During these quieter intervals, lower-energy particles are efficiently deflected away from Earth by the Van Allen radiation belts, zones containing high-energy charged particles trapped by Earth's magnetic field.

As high-energy charged particles enter Earth's upper atmosphere, they spiral downward along magnetic field lines. These energetic particles collide with atmospheric atoms and molecules, causing excitation and ionization. Excited atmospheric species subsequently relax and emit photons (light). Some electrons emitted during these processes can cause secondary ionization of nearby atoms, or, if possessing sufficient kinetic energy, can escape Earth's atmosphere altogether along magnetic field lines.

When auroral activity is exceptionally intense, charged particles may penetrate deeper into the atmosphere, reaching lower layers. At these altitudes, molecular nitrogen (N2) and ionized molecular nitrogen (N2+) dominate the emission of visible auroral light, radiating across a wide range of wavelengths, particularly in the red and blue parts of the visible spectrum. The blue emission at 428 nm is especially pronounced, resulting in vibrant blue and purple auroras at the lower edges of auroral curtains. These molecular nitrogen transitions occur rapidly, as they are quantum mechanically allowed transitions, unlike the slower, forbidden transitions associated with atomic oxygen.

Aurora and the interaction of space particles with Earth's atmosphere are actively studied in Norway. Researchers explore the potential connections between auroral-induced atmospheric chemistry and ozone depletion, investigating whether auroral phenomena may play a role in influencing Earth's climate and potentially contributing to global warming.
